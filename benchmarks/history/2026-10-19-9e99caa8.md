# Cursor benchmark of PyAthena 4.2.0 on 2026-10-19

This entry records a bounded large-data run of the [benchmark harness](../README.md) at commit [`9e99caa8`](https://github.com/pyathena-dev/PyAthena/tree/9e99caa84857f8d29abe63435461fc412e040c43/benchmarks), the commit planned for the PyAthena 4.2.0 release.
PyAthena 4.2.0 was tagged on `baee716c`, which differs from `9e99caa8` only in one test file (#1311), so the measured package code is that of 4.2.0.
The installed distribution reports the version `4.1.1.dev452+g9e99caa84` because its version is derived from the distance to the latest tag.
It repeats the selection of the [4.1.0 run](2026-10-13-437276e1.md) and adds the `temporal` workload (#1287), a scale of 100 million rows (#1162), and every Polars case again with Polars 2.0 (#1292).
The S3 filesystem comparison of the same commit is a [separate entry](2026-10-19-9e99caa8-filesystem.md).

## Selection

The run used 167 jobs without warmups:

- **The 4.1.0 selection**, 96 jobs, as described in the [4.1.0 entry](2026-10-13-437276e1.md#selection) and in [Running on a fleet](../README.md#running-on-a-fleet).
- **The `temporal` workload**, 34 jobs: at large, the row output of Cursor, DictCursor, and S3FSCursor and of the fetch methods of PandasCursor, ArrowCursor, and PolarsCursor at arraysize 1000, every native read of the DataFrame libraries and Wrangler, and `read_sql_query()` through the pandas, Arrow, Polars, and S3FS drivers without chunks and with 100,000-row chunks; at xlarge, the native reads without chunks and with 100,000-row chunks; all with three repetitions.
- **xxlarge (100 million rows)**, 9 jobs, flat: PandasCursor, PolarsCursor, and Wrangler with `chunksize=100000` from every transport, including PandasCursor UNLOAD with `dtype_backend="pyarrow"`, S3FSCursor rows at arraysize 1000, all with three repetitions, and one unchunked PandasCursor CSV read with one repetition.
- **Polars 2.0**, 28 jobs: every `polars` and `sqla_polars` job of the selections above again, with `jobs --environment polars2`.

The configuration matched the 4.1.0 run's, `config.toml` with `chunksizes = [10000, 100000, 1000000]` and `timeout_seconds = 5400.0`, and added the scale `xxlarge = 100000000`.
Only the synchronous API ran.

## Run record

The stack of ten Spot hosts was created at 07:31 UTC, and its hosts started at 07:35 UTC in `us-west-2b` and `us-west-2d`.
Before the queue was published, the group replaced seven of its ten hosts between 08:00 and 08:07 UTC; one of them, the host that was preparing the snapshots, was terminated with the reason `Server.SpotInstanceTermination` at 08:01 UTC, and its preparation and manifest were lost.
The preparation ran again from a local checkout of the same commit with the same configuration against the stack, and the queue `release-1` was published from there at 08:52 UTC.
The snapshot tables and files of the lost preparation were removed before the stack was deleted.

Workers used `--api-slots 10 --terminate-host`.
The first job started at 08:52 UTC and the last finished at 12:05 UTC; every job finished in its first attempt, so no trial was interrupted or superseded.
The longest job was S3FSCursor rows at xxlarge, three trials of about 56 minutes each.

The entry's 367 measured cases, 68 of them in the Polars 2.0 environment, have 1,323 successful trials and no failed trial; 8 cases, Wrangler CSV with the nested shape, are unsupported.
The median Athena queue time of every case was at most 100 ms.
No workflow of this repository ran between 08:52 and 12:05 UTC; the daily database sweep had finished at 08:39 UTC.

## Findings

1. **`pandas.read_sql_query()` through the pandas and Polars drivers was faster than in the 4.1.0 run** (#1167, #1177, #254).
   For 1 million flat rows, `awsathena+pandas` with `unload=true` took 7.02 seconds instead of 18.31, and `awsathena+polars` with `unload=true` 6.66 seconds instead of 11.28; for 10 million flat rows, 50.71 seconds instead of 162.60, and 48.71 seconds instead of 92.05.
   Their client result times fell by 30 to 77% in every case of the two drivers, while `awsathena+arrow` through UNLOAD stayed within 3% of its 4.1.0 time (6.79 and 50.93 seconds).
   Wrangler took 12.37 and 3.89 seconds for 1 million flat rows through CSV and UNLOAD, and 108.88 and 23.44 seconds for 10 million.
2. **Chunked reads of 100 million rows peaked at about the same memory as of 10 million rows** (#1162, #61).
   With `chunksize=100000`, PandasCursor peaked at 462 MiB from CSV results and at 986 MiB (978 MiB with `dtype_backend="pyarrow"`) from UNLOAD results, PolarsCursor at 1,001 to 1,051 MiB with either Polars version, and Wrangler at 390 to 1,173 MiB; the same reads of 10 million rows peaked at 391 to 999 MiB.
   PandasCursor took 699.88 seconds through CSV, against 1,233.06 seconds for Wrangler, and 34.86 to 37.95 seconds through UNLOAD, as did PolarsCursor and Wrangler (35.66 to 41.19 seconds); UNLOAD and CTAS wrote 8 files of up to 184 MiB.
   S3FSCursor returned the 100 million rows in 3,386.53 seconds at 327 MiB, against 323 MiB for 10 million rows.
   The one unchunked PandasCursor CSV read completed in 704.09 seconds at 49,824 MiB, 98.6% of the harness's memory limit of 50,506 MiB (`memory_fraction` 0.8 of the host's memory).
3. **Unchunked PolarsCursor CSV reads with Polars 1.44.2 peaked 25 to 45% higher than in the 4.1.0 run** (#1307).
   At 10 million rows, the peak rose from 6,854 to 9,641 MiB (flat) and from 13,006 to 18,923 MiB (nested); at 1 million rows, from 1,004 to 1,259 MiB and from 1,561 to 2,105 MiB.
   The library versions were the same as in the 4.1.0 run, and the client result times did not change.
   Since #1169, PolarsCursor reads the CSV result file into `bytes` and passes them to `polars.read_csv()`; with Polars 1.x, that holds a copy of the file while it parses.
   With Polars 2.0.0, the same reads peaked at 7,547 and 14,422 MiB at 10 million rows.
4. **Athena took longer to execute the flat CSV query at 10 million rows, so the total times of those cases grew while their client result times did not.**
   The median `TotalExecutionTimeInMillis` of that query was 48.85 seconds, against 28.80 seconds in the 4.1.0 run; the other queries at xlarge took within 9% of their 4.1.0 times.
   The native flat CSV reads of the DataFrame cursors and Wrangler at xlarge took 10 to 61% longer in total, while their client result times changed by +1 to -33%: for example, PolarsCursor with 10,000-row chunks took 54.75 seconds in total instead of 34.10, and 3.67 seconds of client result time instead of 5.48.
5. **CSV reads of PandasCursor, PolarsCursor, and ArrowCursor took less client result time than in the 4.1.0 run.**
   At 10 million rows, PandasCursor's client result time fell from 44.43 to 34.30 seconds without chunks (flat), PolarsCursor's from 5.22 to 5.48 seconds to 3.67 to 3.82 seconds with chunks (flat), and ArrowCursor's from 16.84 to 10.95 seconds (nested).
   The cause of these differences was not investigated.
6. **With the `temporal` workload, CSV reads and row output took several times the client result time of the flat workload** (#1287, #1308).
   For 1 million rows from CSV results, Cursor took 304.91 seconds of client result time against 217.62 seconds for the flat workload, S3FSCursor 88.53 against 29.28 seconds, and the fetch methods of PandasCursor, ArrowCursor, and PolarsCursor 26.79, 37.85, and 33.95 seconds, against 1.96, 3.36, and 1.53 seconds from UNLOAD results, which hold no time zone or `time` columns.
   PandasCursor's native CSV read took 24.04 seconds against 3.80 seconds for the flat workload, and Wrangler's 29.30 against 6.02 seconds.
   At 10 million rows, PandasCursor's CSV read with 100,000-row chunks took 163.71 seconds of client result time, against 76.50 seconds without chunks; Wrangler took 264.93 and 154.22 seconds.
   UNLOAD and CTAS reads, which add only `event_time` and `event_date`, took within 0.3 seconds of the flat workload's client result time at 1 million rows.
7. **Polars 2.0.0 measured like Polars 1.44.2 except for unchunked CSV reads.**
   Across the 68 cases of both environments, median total times agreed within 11%, and client result times and peaks within 10%, with these exceptions, each given for Polars 2.0.0 against 1.44.2:
   - Unchunked CSV reads peaked lower at large nested and at xlarge (7,547 against 9,641 MiB for 10 million flat rows), and higher at medium (327 against 260 MiB).
   - Chunked CSV reads at xlarge took 8.37 to 8.64 seconds of client result time against 7.04 to 7.26 seconds (nested, 10,000 and 100,000-row chunks), and 3.35 to 3.43 against 3.76 to 3.82 seconds (flat, 100,000 and 1,000,000-row chunks).
   - At 100 million rows, the chunked CSV read took 33.82 seconds of client result time against 41.04 seconds.
8. **Row output through GetQueryResults and S3FSCursor stayed within 7% of the 4.1.0 run.**
   Cursor took 224.82 seconds for 1 million flat rows at arraysize 1000 (226.46 in the 4.1.0 run) and 1,013.56 seconds at arraysize 100 (1,062.61), DictCursor 216.81 seconds (230.44), and S3FSCursor 35.46 seconds (36.69), with the same peaks.

## Limits

- Only the synchronous API ran.
- xxlarge covered the flat shape only, without ArrowCursor, and with one trial of the unchunked PandasCursor read.
- The `temporal` workload has no 4.1.0 measurement; its comparisons use the flat workload of the same run, whose CSV results are smaller: Athena took 101.59 seconds to execute the temporal CSV query at 10 million rows, against 48.85 seconds for the flat one.
  The local before-and-after measurements of the temporal conversion are in the pull requests that #1287 lists.
- Polars 2.0 ran the Polars families only; the other families ran with Polars 1.44.2 installed.
- Three or five trials per case make p95 values descriptive.
- Times include Athena execution and one-second polling; compare client result times for the client's share, as Athena's execution time of the same query changed between the runs (finding 4).
- Comparisons with the 4.1.0 run use the same instance type, snapshots' row counts, and selection, but different hosts, days, and snapshots of the same source partition.
- Peak RSS is sampled every 50 ms and can miss short peaks.
- Results from other instance types or architectures are not directly comparable.
